# Product Tone of Voice Guideline
## A Dota-native voice system derived from the supplied Dota 2 News articles

**Version:** 1.1
**Purpose:** Editorial, product UI, player insights, email, chat, notifications, social/share surfaces, support, and AI-generated copy  

---

# 1. Why this guide exists

This product should sound like it belongs in the world of Dota without sounding like a wiki, a generic esports publication, a growth-marketing funnel, or a player trying too hard to be funny.

The source articles consistently do two jobs at once:

1. **Tell people something useful.**
2. **Make the telling feel like Dota.**

The useful information remains legible: dates, rules, ticket restrictions, patch changes, tournament formats, calls to action, and feature descriptions are concrete and easy to find.

The personality appears around that information: dry understatement, dramatic verbs, knowing asides, self-aware exaggeration, playful framing, and references that assume the reader already understands what kind of strange game Dota is.

That relationship is the foundation of this guideline.

> **Utility first. Personality second. Dota flavor throughout.**

Do not write every sentence as a joke. Do not strip every sentence down until it sounds like enterprise software. The target is **clear information delivered by a writer who knows the game, knows the audience, and is allowed to have a personality.**

---

# 2. The voice in one sentence

> **Write like a sharp, Dota-literate narrator who respects the player’s intelligence, enjoys the absurdity of the game, and knows when to stop joking and give them the information they came for.**

If a shorter operating instruction is needed:

> **Clear first. Dota-aware second. Dryly playful when earned. Never try-hard.**

---

# 3. Voice principles

## 3.1 We are in on it

The source voice rarely explains Dota from the outside. It writes from inside the culture.

It assumes:

- Dota is complicated.
- Players are opinionated.
- Matches get messy.
- Predictions go wrong.
- People will spend unreasonable amounts of attention on small details.
- Everyone involved has, at some point, made a questionable decision.
- This is part of the fun.

The writer and reader are on the same side of the joke.

### Do

- Refer to familiar player behavior without overexplaining it.
- Use Dota vocabulary when it is the shortest and most natural word.
- Make the player feel recognized.
- Treat the game's complexity as familiar territory, not a defect that requires apology.

**Example**

> You call it “one more game.” Your match history calls it six.

**Example**

> Your safest hero is also the one you keep picking into terrible matchups. Commitment is one word for it.

### Don’t

- Define common Dota terms for experienced players unless the surface is specifically beginner-facing.
- Stuff copy with references just to prove the writer knows Dota.
- Use stale gamer slang as a substitute for wit.
- write like a spectator describing “the Dota community” from the outside.

**Too generic**

> Players often choose comfort heroes when they want to improve their chance of winning.

**Better**

> When the draft gets uncomfortable, you go back to the same three heroes. Usually, they return the favor.

---

## 3.2 Clear facts are sacred

The strongest source articles can be playful in one paragraph and ruthlessly practical in the next. When the reader needs a date, price, restriction, rule, status, or instruction, the copy becomes direct.

Personality must never obscure:

- money
- deadlines
- eligibility
- destructive actions
- account state
- errors
- privacy
- permissions
- cancellations
- limitations
- data confidence
- technical requirements

### Do

> Your analysis uses your last 200 public matches.

> We couldn't read this match because OpenDota has not parsed it.

> Delete report? This removes the report from your account. Your Dota match history is unaffected.

### Don’t

> The Ancient archives are being coy today. Perhaps the match gods will reveal your numbers later.

This is cute and useless. If the request failed, say what failed.

### Rule

**The more consequential the message, the less decorative the language.**

---

## 3.3 Humor is dry, not loud

The source corpus uses humor frequently, but rarely through punchlines, memes, emoji, or internet catchphrases. The comedy usually comes from **contrast**:

- treating something absurd as ordinary;
- treating something ordinary as grand;
- inserting one unnecessary-but-specific aside;
- acknowledging what the reader is probably thinking;
- escalating a premise one step past reason;
- using an unexpectedly formal phrase for a silly situation.

The best joke feels discovered inside the sentence, not attached afterward.

### Pattern: absurd thing, calm reaction

> You bought nine Sentries in 34 minutes. Vision was not going to be the reason this game went badly.

### Pattern: grand language, small reality

> At minute 18, your noble campaign for map control encountered a significant obstacle: three consecutive deaths.

### Pattern: parenthetical aside

> Your farming improved after minute 20 (once everyone stopped trying to kill you, which helped).

### Pattern: acknowledge the obvious

> Your best win rate comes from a hero you barely play. Naturally.

### Avoid

- “LOL”
- “LMAO”
- “bro”
- “skill issue”
- “touch grass”
- “cooked”
- “goated”
- random all-caps
- reaction GIF language
- ironic emoji
- jokes that insult the player
- references likely to expire in six months

A rare community phrase can be used when the product context strongly supports it, but the default voice should survive longer than a meme cycle.

---

## 3.4 Drama comes from verbs, not hype adjectives

The tournament writing in the sources feels dramatic because actions are concrete: teams **claw back**, **tear through**, **survive**, **stave off elimination**, **punch a ticket**, **fight through**, **raise the Aegis**.

It does not rely primarily on words like:

- incredible
- amazing
- epic
- insane
- legendary
- mind-blowing

Prefer a vivid action to an empty adjective.

### Weak

> You had an amazing performance in the late game.

### Better

> Once the clock passed 35 minutes, your games stopped slipping away. You won 68% of the matches that reached late game.

### Weak

> An incredible comeback!

### Better

> You were 9k down at 31:00. Twelve minutes later, you were hitting the Ancient.

---

## 3.5 The reader is “you,” not a demographic

The source voice speaks directly to the audience. It uses “you,” “your,” “we,” and “us” naturally.

This creates companionship without forced friendliness.

### Prefer

> You survive bad lanes better than most players in your bracket.

### Over

> Players with similar behavioral characteristics may demonstrate increased resilience following a negative laning phase.

Use the second form only when analytical precision genuinely requires it.

---

## 3.6 Specificity makes the voice

The source writing gets much of its charm from specific details. “A massive fully-voiced story featuring a spider-legged convoy of deadly robots” is more alive than “an exciting new story.”

For this product, use the data.

### Generic

> You are an aggressive player.

### Specific

> In winning games, you join your first kill 2.8 minutes earlier than in losses.

### Generic + decorative

> You are a fearless battlefield commander who charges into battle!

### Specific + voice

> You don't wait for fights to find you. Your first involvement comes earlier than most players we analyzed — and sometimes earlier than your team was ready for.

The second version creates identity **and** earns it with evidence.

---

## 3.7 Respect the player

The source voice teases the audience, but it does not sneer at them.

The product can point out:

- greed
- overextension
- passive play
- bad habits
- narrow hero pools
- risky drafts
- inconsistency
- low-impact patterns

But the copy should describe behavior, not assign character flaws.

### Good

> You tend to stay for one more wave after a won fight. Sometimes that becomes a tower. Sometimes it becomes your death recap.

### Bad

> You're greedy and have terrible game sense.

### Good

> When games go badly, your warding drops sharply after minute 25.

### Bad

> You give up when you're losing.

Infer only what the data supports.

# 4. Tone sliders

These are default levels, not fixed values.

| Dimension | Default | Guidance |
|---|---:|---|
| Clarity | 5/5 | Never sacrifice it |
| Dota fluency | 4/5 | Natural, not encyclopedic |
| Playfulness | 3/5 | Present but controlled |
| Dry humor | 3/5 | Often one beat, not a routine |
| Drama | 2/5 | Raise for milestones and narratives |
| Warmth | 3/5 | Companionable, not sentimental |
| Technical precision | 4/5 | Especially for data and systems |
| Hype | 1/5 | Earned moments only |
| Corporate polish | 1/5 | Avoid brochure language |
| Meme energy | 1/5 | Rare, highly contextual |
| Snark | 1/5 | Aim at situations, not people |
| Lore/theatricality | 2/5 | Accent, never camouflage |

# 5. Tone changes by situation

The **voice stays the same**. The **tone changes** according to what is happening.

## 5.1 Celebration

Use more drama, momentum, and vivid verbs.

**Example**

> You found your closer. Over your last 50 matches, Lina turned close games into wins more reliably than anyone else in your pool.

Avoid fake confetti language:

> Amazing! You're absolutely CRUSHING it! 🎉🔥

---

## 5.2 Discovery

This is a core product moment. Create the feeling of “I didn't know that.”

Structure:

1. observation;
2. tension or contrast;
3. evidence;
4. implication.

**Example**

> **You play faster when you're behind.**  
> In wins, your first major rotation lands around 12:40. In losses, it moves up to 10:55. Falling behind doesn't make you retreat — it makes you look for a fight.

The surprising interpretation should emerge from the relationship between data points, not from exaggerated wording.

---

## 5.3 Improvement

Be candid, specific, and constructive.

**Example**

> Your deaths aren't unusually high overall. The timing is the problem. Nearly a third happen in the three minutes after your team wins a fight, when the map is temporarily yours to use. Bank the win before looking for the next one.

Avoid coach cosplay:

> You MUST fix your positioning if you ever want to climb.

---

## 5.4 Failure or error

Drop most humor. Say:

1. what happened;
2. what it affects;
3. what the user can do.

**Example**

> We couldn't load your recent matches from OpenDota. Your Steam ID is valid, but the match request failed. Try again in a few minutes.

Optional lightness may appear only if nothing consequential is at stake:

> No matches found. Either this account is very new, very private, or impressively good at hiding.

But provide the actual explanation immediately.

---

## 5.5 Loading and waiting

A small amount of flavor is useful because the user has no action to take.

Rotate through messages that reflect real system stages when possible.

**Good**

> Pulling your recent matches…

> Looking for patterns that survive more than one bad Tuesday…

> Comparing heroes, roles, timings, and outcomes…

> Separating habits from coincidences…

Avoid fake technical theater:

> Calibrating the Ancient neural lattice…

unless the system is intentionally presented as fictional and the real state remains clear elsewhere.

---

## 5.6 High-stakes or irreversible action

No jokes.

**Example**

> Delete this report?  
> This permanently removes the saved report from your account. You can generate a new one later.

---

# 6. Sentence-level style

## 6.1 Use conversational contractions

Prefer:

- we're
- you've
- don't
- can't
- it's
- you'll

over needlessly formal alternatives.

**Stiff**

> We have identified a pattern in which you do not frequently rotate before ten minutes.

**Natural**

> We've found one pattern: you don't rotate much before 10:00.

---

## 6.2 Vary sentence length

A common source rhythm is:

- medium explanatory sentence;
- longer sentence with detail;
- short landing sentence.

**Example**

> You play a wide hero pool, but your wins come from a surprisingly narrow part of it. Across 18 heroes, four account for nearly half your victories. Your comfort zone is bigger than it looks.

Short sentences should land an idea, not become the entire voice.

---

## 6.3 Use parentheses for the second voice in the writer’s head

Parentheses are one of the most recognizable techniques in the source corpus.

Use them for:

- a dry correction;
- a useful caveat;
- an absurd implication;
- a small contextual aside.

**Example**

> Your average fight participation climbs after 30 minutes (which is helpful, because by then hiding becomes considerably harder).

Do not stack multiple parentheticals in one paragraph.

---

## 6.4 Use em dashes for turns

Em dashes work well when the sentence pivots.

**Example**

> You don't avoid risky fights — you avoid *unplanned* ones.

Do not turn every sentence into an em-dash construction.

---

## 6.5 Rhetorical questions: one, then answer it

The source articles sometimes frame information as the reader's question.

**Example**

> So where are those extra deaths coming from? Mostly the five minutes after your first tower falls.

Useful in articles, FAQs, and insight sequences.

Less useful in tiny UI labels.

---

## 6.6 Prefer concrete nouns and verbs

Prefer:

- fight
- lane
- ward
- tower
- stack
- smoke
- rotate
- farm
- die
- retreat
- buy
- pick
- win
- lose

over:

- engagement
- resource acquisition
- strategic positioning behavior
- performance outcome

Use analytical terminology only when it makes a distinction the ordinary Dota word cannot.

---

## 6.7 Numbers should do work

Never dump a number and expect it to become insight.

Bad:

> Fight participation: 63%

Better:

> You're in 63% of your team's kills — high for your role, but most of that involvement arrives after the first 15 minutes.

Better still:

> You look passive in lane, then suddenly appear everywhere. Only 31% of your kill involvement happens before 15:00; after that, you're in nearly three out of every four fights.

# 7. Vocabulary

## 7.1 Preferred vocabulary

Use ordinary Dota words naturally:

- lane / laning
- rotate / rotation
- farm
- fight / teamfight
- gank
- ward / vision
- smoke
- stack
- objective
- tower
- Roshan / Rosh
- high ground
- buyback
- draft
- hero pool
- comfort hero
- tempo
- map
- Ancient
- support / core / mid / offlane / safelane
- comeback
- stomp
- throw, only when the evidence and context justify it

## 7.2 Use analytical words carefully

Useful:

- pattern
- signal
- tendency
- consistency
- volatility
- sample
- confidence
- correlation
- compared with
- relative to
- over your last X matches
- in games where…

Avoid jargon unless necessary:

- behavioral vector
- latent dimension
- multivariate feature
- normalized score
- percentile rank

Those may exist inside the system. They rarely belong in player-facing copy.

# 8. Humor mechanics

## 8.1 Understatement

State a dramatic situation as if it is a minor inconvenience.

> Dying twice before the first rune is, statistically speaking, not an ideal opening.

Use sparingly. The deadpan delivery is the joke.

---

## 8.2 Overstatement

Treat a small player habit like a historical campaign.

> Your long-running diplomatic dispute with Observer Wards continues.

Only use when the underlying statistic is real.

---

## 8.3 The knowingly unnecessary aside

> You bought a Gem in six of these matches (and kept it alive in four, which is really the important part).

---

## 8.4 Formal language for ridiculous things

> Your attempt to solo Roshan at 17:42 was ambitious. The subsequent proceedings were brief.

---

## 8.5 Reader complicity

> You know the game: the enemy carry disappears for two minutes, everyone pings Roshan, and somehow all five of you still arrive separately.

This should sound like shared experience, not a lecture.

---

## 8.6 Reversal

Set up the expected interpretation, then overturn it with data.

> Your deaths look aggressive. They aren't. Most happen while retreating from fights you joined late.

This is especially valuable for “discovery” insights.

---

# 9. Humor boundaries

Do **not** make jokes about:

- payment failures;
- bans or account penalties;
- privacy;
- lost data;
- security;
- serious harassment;
- discrimination;
- mental health;
- addiction;
- spending;
- a person's intelligence;
- disability;
- nationality, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexuality, or other identity;
- real-world tragedy;
- user support issues that have already caused frustration.

Do not use humiliation as motivation.

A player can laugh at a pattern. They should not feel the product is laughing at them.

# 10. Product insight writing system

This section is especially important for a Dota analysis product.

A useful insight should usually contain four layers.

## 10.1 Layer 1 — The hook

One clear claim.

> **You don't tilt into passivity. You speed up.**

Not:

> **Aggression Under Adversity Score: 82**

Scores can support the claim, but the human idea comes first.

---

## 10.2 Layer 2 — The evidence

Show the strongest proof.

> After losing your lane, your first rotation happens 1:46 earlier and your fight participation rises 11 points over the next ten minutes.

Use enough evidence to establish credibility, not every available metric.

---

## 10.3 Layer 3 — The interpretation

Explain what the data relationship suggests.

> Falling behind seems to trigger action rather than caution. That makes you dangerous in unstable games, but it also means one bad lane can pull you into fights before your timings are ready.

Use calibrated language:

- “suggests”
- “tends to”
- “in this sample”
- “we see”
- “appears”
- “is consistent with”

when the data does not justify certainty.

---

## 10.4 Layer 4 — The move

Give one practical next step.

> **Try this:** after a lost lane, check your next item timing before joining the first available fight. If it's under 90 seconds away, finish it first unless your team is defending an objective.

Actionable means the player can do something in the next match.

---

# 11. Insight headlines

The best headline is a **human observation**, not a metric label.

## Good

- You get bolder when the game gets worse.
- Your safest hero isn't your best hero.
- You farm like a carry and fight like a support.
- Your first death changes the way you play.
- You win the boring games.
- You are much better from ahead than you think.
- Your hero pool has a hidden center.
- You don't lose lanes often. You lose them expensively.
- You are strongest when someone else starts the fight.
- Your comeback games have one thing in common.

## Weak

- Aggression Analysis
- Hero Performance
- Farming Statistics
- Behavioral Insight #4
- Tempo Index
- Match Findings

A category label may sit above the headline, but should not replace it.

---

# 12. Archetype and trait copy

Archetypes should feel recognizable, not horoscope-like.

## Structure

**Archetype name**  
Short identity statement.

**What it means**  
Two or three behavioral patterns that combine into the archetype.

**Why we think so**  
Evidence across multiple dimensions.

**The upside**  
Where the style works.

**The tension**  
Where the same style can become a weakness.

**Watch for**  
One practical behavior.

### Example

## The Second-Wave Hunter

You are rarely the first body into a fight. You are often the one who makes the fight decisive.

Across your recent matches, your strongest results come when another teammate initiates and you enter several seconds later. Your deaths stay lower, your kill participation rises, and your damage becomes more efficient.

**The upside:** you read developing fights well and punish overcommitment.

**The tension:** when nobody on your team wants to start, you can wait too long and surrender the map.

**Watch for:** lineups where your team has no obvious initiator. Those are the games where you may need to become one.

### Avoid

> You are the Second-Wave Hunter, a mysterious and calculating warrior who lurks in the shadows before striking with deadly precision.

This is flavor without evidence.

---

# 13. Confidence language

The product must distinguish observation from inference.

## High confidence

Use when repeated and directly measurable.

> You pick Puck more than any other mid hero.

> Your win rate drops in matches where your first death happens before 8:00.

## Medium confidence

> You tend to recover from weak lanes by joining fights earlier.

> Your recent matches suggest you are becoming less dependent on one comfort hero.

## Low confidence / exploratory

> There may be a pattern here: in this sample, your best games start with unusually low early farm but high assist involvement. We need more matches before calling it a stable trait.

Never turn a weak signal into a personality diagnosis.

---

# 14. Comparison language

Comparisons need a visible reference point.

Bad:

> Your warding is excellent.

Better:

> You place more Observer Wards than most position 5 players in this sample.

Better:

> You place 2.1 more Observer Wards per 30 minutes than comparable position 5 players — but deward less often.

If the comparison cohort is weak or unavailable, compare the player to themselves:

> On your five most-played supports, your vision score is 18% higher in wins than losses.

---

# 15. Long-form web articles

## 15.1 Recommended structure

### 1. Lead with a scene, tension, or useful premise

Do not start with administrative throat-clearing.

Weak:

> We are excited to announce the release of our new player analysis feature.

Better:

> Most Dota players can tell you their best hero. Far fewer can tell you what they do differently when a game starts going wrong.

Then explain the feature.

### 2. State the news

Within the first one or two paragraphs, tell the reader what changed or what exists.

### 3. Explain why it matters

Connect the feature to player behavior or a familiar problem.

### 4. Break down details

Use descriptive subheads.

### 5. Finish with a specific action

> Run a new report and see which patterns survived your last 50 matches.

not:

> Learn more today.

---

## 15.2 Article headline style

Prefer informative headlines with occasional personality.

Good:

- Your Dota DNA Now Tracks How You Recover From Bad Lanes
- We Added Role-Specific Comparisons
- Match Stories: A Better Way to Understand the Games Behind Your Numbers
- Your Report Just Got More Opinionated

Avoid clickbait:

- This INSANE New Feature Will Change How You Play Dota Forever

---

# 16. UI copy

UI copy has less room for theatricality. Compress the voice, not the clarity.

## 16.1 Buttons

Use direct verbs.

Good:

- Analyze matches
- Build my report
- See the evidence
- Show matches
- Compare roles
- Try another ID
- Share result
- Save report
- Continue

Avoid:

- Proceed
- Submit
- Execute analysis
- Discover now!
- Unlock the magic

unless the actual action is “unlock.”

---

## 16.2 Onboarding

### Hero

> **Your match history knows more about your playstyle than your MMR does.**  
> We analyze your recent Dota matches to find patterns in how you draft, farm, fight, recover, and win.

CTA: **Analyze my matches**

### Supporting note

> Uses public match data. No Steam password required.

---

## 16.3 Input helper text

> Paste a Steam ID, profile URL, or OpenDota player URL.

Error:

> We couldn't find a player from that ID. Check the number or paste the full profile URL.

Do not write:

> Invalid input.

when the product can explain what is valid.

---

## 16.4 Empty state

> **Nothing here yet.**  
> Run your first analysis and we'll start looking for the habits hiding between the obvious stats.

CTA: **Analyze a player**

---

## 16.5 Loading

Use a small pool of messages tied to real processing stages.

- Fetching recent matches…
- Grouping your heroes and roles…
- Looking for patterns across wins and losses…
- Checking whether the pattern survives more than a few matches…
- Turning suspiciously large piles of numbers into sentences…

Avoid showing ten whimsical lines before the result. One or two is enough.

---

## 16.6 Success

> **Report ready.**  
> We found 12 stable patterns across 196 recent matches.

CTA: **Meet your playstyle**

If the product cannot justify “stable,” use “patterns worth showing.”

---

## 16.7 Error

> **We couldn't finish this report.**  
> OpenDota stopped responding while we were fetching match data. Your progress wasn't charged. Try again.

If payment is involved, explicitly state the payment state.

---

## 16.8 Tooltip

Short, literal, and useful.

> **Fight participation**  
> The percentage of your team's hero kills where you got a kill or assist.

Optional contextual sentence:

> Useful, but role and game length matter.

---

# 17. Emails

Email can carry more personality than UI, but the inbox still rewards clarity.

## 17.1 Subject lines

Informative first.

Good:

- Your Dota report is ready
- We found a new pattern in your last 50 matches
- Your report changed after 27 new games
- New: role-specific comparisons
- Your safest hero is not who we expected

Avoid:

- 🚨 YOU WON'T BELIEVE THIS DOTA INSIGHT 🚨
- Huge News!!!
- An Exciting Update From Our Team
- [Company] Product Newsletter — August Edition

---

## 17.2 Report-ready email

**Subject:** Your Dota report is ready

> We finished going through your recent matches.
>
> The headline: you are far more aggressive after a bad lane than after a good one. That sounds backwards until you look at what happens next.
>
> We found 11 other patterns covering your hero pool, tempo, teamfights, recovery, and closing habits.
>
> **See my report**
>
> Your report used 198 public matches available through OpenDota.

---

## 17.3 Feature announcement email

Start with the player problem, not the company milestone.

Weak:

> We are thrilled to announce that our team has launched an exciting new comparison feature.

Better:

> A 58% win rate means very little if you don't know what you're being compared with.
>
> Reports can now compare your patterns with players in the same role, so “high,” “low,” and “unusual” finally have a useful reference point.

---

## 17.4 Transactional email

Keep it plain.

**Subject:** Payment received

> Your payment for Deep Analysis was successful.
>
> **Order:** Deep Analysis  
> **Amount:** Rp29.000  
> **Status:** Paid
>
> Your report is now being generated. We'll email you when it is ready.

No jokes around charges or receipts.

# 18. Chat and conversational surfaces

Chat should feel quick, competent, and Dota-literate.

## Default behavior

- Answer the question first.
- Use short paragraphs.
- Match the user's level of Dota knowledge.
- Explain uncertainty.
- Do not roleplay a coach unless asked.
- Do not flood the reply with jokes.
- Ask a follow-up only when it materially improves the next answer.

### Example: “Why is my archetype The Anchor?”

> Because three patterns line up: you die less than your teammates in losing games, your fight participation stays stable after 25 minutes, and your hero pool skews toward heroes that protect or reset fights.
>
> The interesting part is that this isn't just “you play support.” The same pattern still appears in your offlane matches.

### Example: “So am I passive?”

> Not overall. You're selective.
>
> You join fewer early fights than comparable players, but once you commit, your kill participation is high. The weakness shows up when your team needs someone to start the action and nobody does.

---

# 19. Notifications and push

Notifications have almost no room. One idea only.

Good:

> Your report changed: a new late-game pattern showed up.

> 23 new matches are ready to analyze.

> Your Deep Analysis is ready.

Potentially playful, low-stakes:

> Your hero pool has been up to something. New report ready.

Avoid fake urgency.

> URGENT: Your Dota DNA changed!

---

# 20. Share cards and social copy

Shareable copy should describe the person, not advertise the product.

## Good card

**THE SECOND-WAVE HUNTER**  
You rarely start the fight. You are often the reason it ends.

72% fight participation after 25:00  
Low first-death rate  
High cleanup efficiency

## Weak card

**I USED [PRODUCT] TO ANALYZE MY DOTA PERSONALITY!**

The player's identity is the content. The product mark can be secondary.

# 21. Paywalls, upgrades, and monetization

The voice must not manipulate the user.

## Good

> **Want the match-level explanation?**  
> Free analysis shows the pattern. Deep Analysis selects the matches most likely to explain *why* it happens and inspects them in detail.

CTA: **See Deep Analysis**

## Good locked insight

> **You change after your first death.**  
> We found a repeatable shift in your next ten minutes of play. Deep Analysis shows which decisions change and the matches where it is clearest.

## Bad

> Unlock your TRUE Dota personality before it's too late.

## Bad

> Serious players choose Deep Analysis.

Do not imply that paying makes someone more legitimate.

---

# 22. Support and service communication

When someone has a problem, usefulness outranks brand voice.

## Good

> Your payment succeeded, but the report generation failed before any paid match parsing began. You haven't lost the analysis credit. You can retry from the report page.

## Bad

> Looks like the courier dropped your report somewhere on the way to base.

The second line may be acceptable as an optional flourish **after** the real explanation, but it adds little.

---

# 23. Patch notes and changelogs

The source corpus shows that not every update needs a story.

For product changelogs:

- state the version or date;
- summarize major changes;
- list specific changes;
- explain migration or behavior changes;
- keep jokes rare.

### Example

# August 17 Update

We've made player reports easier to trust and easier to scan.

- Added sample-size labels to trait cards.
- Added role-specific comparisons for support, offlane, mid, and carry.
- Reports now flag insights based on fewer than 10 relevant matches.
- Fixed a case where Turbo matches could enter the ranked-match comparison set.
- Improved retry behavior when OpenDota returns partial match data.

And yes, the “92% warding percentile” from six Turbo games has been sent back to wherever it came from.

One closing joke is enough.

# 24. FAQ style

The question can sound like the user. The answer should be straightforward.

### Does this tell me my real personality?

No. It describes patterns in how you play Dota. We may use personality-like language to make those patterns easier to recognize, but we are not making psychological or clinical claims.

### Why did my archetype change?

Your report reflects the matches in its sample. If your hero pool, role, patch, or recent habits change enough, the strongest pattern can change with them.

### Is a high score always good?

No. Most traits are tradeoffs. High aggression can create tempo or feed kills. High consistency can mean reliability or predictability. The report should explain what the score appears to do in your games.

# 25. Localization and Indonesian-language guidance

If the product is localized into Bahasa Indonesia, translate the **attitude**, not the English syntax.

Preserve:

- directness;
- dry understatement;
- player-to-player familiarity;
- concise Dota terminology;
- jokes built on situation rather than wordplay.

Do not force every Dota term into formal Indonesian if the player base normally uses the English term.

Likely natural:

- lane
- farm
- ward
- rotate / rotasi
- fight
- draft
- pick
- support
- carry
- offlane
- mid
- high ground
- buyback
- timing

Choose one local convention and remain consistent.

### English

> You stay one wave too long after won fights.

### Natural Indonesian direction

> Habis menang fight, kamu sering masih ambil satu wave lagi. Kadang dapat tower. Kadang malah masuk death recap.

Avoid overly formal localization:

> Setelah memenangkan pertarungan tim, Anda cenderung melakukan akuisisi satu gelombang creep tambahan.

For Indonesian copy, “kamu” versus “Anda” is a product-level decision. Do not switch between them casually. For a Dota-native consumer product, “kamu” will generally support this voice better; transactional/legal surfaces may require a more neutral construction rather than suddenly changing persona.

# 26. Accessibility and inclusion

Clarity helps everyone.

- Do not rely on color alone to communicate positive/negative.
- Expand uncommon abbreviations on first use when appropriate.
- Keep tooltips factual.
- Do not use jokes as the only explanation.
- Avoid culturally narrow metaphors when the same message must work globally.
- Do not assume gender.
- Do not equate rank with intelligence, worth, effort, or legitimacy.
- Do not shame people for playing casually, playing Turbo, taking breaks, or having a small match sample.

# 27. Editorial mechanics

## Capitalization

Use normal sentence case for most UI and headings unless visual design defines another system.

Prefer:

> Analyze my matches

not:

> Analyze My Matches

Official names keep official capitalization:

- Dota 2
- The International
- OpenDota
- Steam
- Roshan
- Aegis of Champions

---

## Punctuation

- Contractions: encouraged.
- Em dash: useful for pivots, use in moderation.
- Parentheses: encouraged for concise asides.
- Exclamation mark: rare.
- Ellipsis: rare and intentional.
- Multiple exclamation marks: never.
- Emoji: generally avoid in core product voice.
- Semicolons: fine, but plain sentences are usually better.

---

## Numerals

Use numerals for data:

- 12 matches
- 63%
- 14:32
- 2.3 deaths
- 5k gold lead

Be consistent with time notation.

When precision does not matter, natural phrasing is allowed:

> almost half your games

When the insight depends on precision, show the number.

# 32. AI-agent writing instructions

Use this block as a reusable instruction for AI agents generating product copy.

## System / style prompt

```text
Write in our Dota-native product voice.

CORE VOICE
- Clear, direct, Dota-literate, dryly playful, and specific.
- Sound like a knowledgeable narrator who is in on the game with the reader.
- Treat the reader as intelligent. Use "you" naturally.
- Utility comes before personality.
- Use humor as an occasional aside, reversal, understatement, or exaggerated framing. Do not write punchlines for their own sake.
- Prefer concrete Dota verbs and observable behavior over abstract labels.
- Prefer evidence-backed human claims over metric names.
- Use contractions.
- Vary sentence length.
- Parenthetical asides and em-dash pivots are allowed in moderation.
- Exclamation marks and emoji are rare.
- Avoid generic corporate, marketing, and AI-generated phrasing.

DATA / INSIGHT RULES
- Never claim more than the data supports.
- Separate fact from interpretation.
- Use calibrated language for inference: "tends to", "suggests", "in this sample", "we see".
- Include relevant sample size or comparison basis when it affects trust.
- Never infer psychology, mental state, intelligence, or moral character from gameplay data.
- Describe tradeoffs rather than declaring traits universally good or bad.
- Convert numbers into relationships, contrasts, or consequences.
- Give one practical action when the surface is about improvement.

HUMOR RULES
- Joke about the situation, the game, or a recognizable Dota habit — not the person's worth.
- No memes or slang just to sound young.
- No humor around payments, privacy, security, bans, lost data, harassment, or destructive actions.
- One good joke is better than five mediocre ones.
- If a sentence is already interesting because the data is surprising, do not add a joke.

CHANNEL CALIBRATION
- UI: shortest and clearest version.
- Insight cards: claim → evidence → interpretation → action.
- Articles: more room for narrative openings and asides.
- Email: informative subject, personality in the body.
- Chat: answer first; concise, contextual follow-up.
- Errors/support/transactional: mostly plain language.
- Share cards: identity statement plus evidence; minimal product promotion.

AVOID
"unlock your potential", "level up", "game-changing", "seamless",
"powerful insights", "embark", "elevate", "unleash", "delve",
"tapestry", "multifaceted", "it is important to note",
unearned "amazing", "incredible", "epic", "insane",
and gamer taunts such as "skill issue" or "touch grass".

FINAL SELF-CHECK
Before returning copy, ask:
1. Is the useful fact immediately understandable?
2. Does the copy sound like it knows Dota rather than merely mentioning Dota?
3. Is every interpretation supported by evidence?
4. Did I use specificity instead of hype?
5. Is any joke earned and safe?
6. Could I delete one decorative phrase and make this stronger?
7. Is the call to action literal?
```

# 35. Fast quality test: “Could this be from any app?”

Take the copy and replace “Dota” with another game.

If it still works perfectly, it may be too generic.

### Too generic

> Discover insights that help you understand your unique playstyle.

This could belong to any analytics product.

### Dota-native

> Your MMR tells you where you landed. Your match history tells us how you keep getting there.

This is still broadly understandable, but the framing belongs to the problem the product is solving.

Do not solve genericness by adding random hero names. Solve it by talking about **real Dota decisions and real player tensions**.

---

# 36. Fast quality test: “Is this trying too hard?”

Signs the copy is trying too hard:

- every paragraph has a joke;
- every noun has an adjective;
- three Dota references appear where one would do;
- the copy performs gamer identity instead of conveying information;
- the writer sounds pleased with the joke;
- an insight is weaker after the joke is removed;
- slang is carrying the personality.

Fix by deleting 30% of the decoration and making the observation more specific.

---

# 37. Fast quality test: “Where is the evidence?”

For every insight, underline the claim.

Then ask:

> What data would I show if the player challenged this sentence?

If the answer is unclear, one of three things is wrong:

1. the claim is too broad;
2. the evidence is missing;
3. the sentence is pretending interpretation is fact.

Rewrite before shipping.

# 41. Free DNA identity cadence

Free DNA is a personal identity read, not an analytics worksheet. The report
can qualify findings with private gates, but the player-facing sentence should
lead with what feels recognisable in their game. The reader should meet a
conclusion before they meet a measurement.

## The five-beat cadence

Use this order when a finding has enough shape to show:

1. **Personal conclusion.** Say the thing the player can recognise in one
   breath: “You keep arriving before the fight becomes somebody else’s
   problem.”
2. **Recognisable explanation.** Name the behaviour or contrast that gives the
   conclusion its shape, using Dota language where it is the natural word.
3. **Optional vivid personal statistic.** Include one number only when it makes
   the identity more memorable. A number is a spotlight, not a receipt.
4. **Useful implication.** Give the player one action, draft question, or
   decision to try next.
5. **Optional dry turn.** Add one calm, specific aside when the situation has
   earned it: “Whether that is helpful depends, rather inconveniently, on what
   happens next.”

Every chapter may earn roughly one dry beat. A finding does not need all five
beats when a shorter line is clearer; the order is the default, not a quota.

## Named anti-patterns

- **The evidence dump:** listing receipts, denominators, sample counts, or
  source explanations before saying what the player should notice.
- **Confidence cosplay:** turning internal qualification, confidence, or
  coverage gates into visible labels that make a conclusion sound bureaucratic.
- **The methodology throat-clear:** explaining how the report was assembled
  when the player asked what their game looks like.
- **The verdict voice:** converting one pattern into a judgement about the
  player’s character, skill, mood, or intent.
- **The template chorus:** repeating “What we saw,” “Why it matters,” and “What
  this actually means” for every card. Give each finding its own heading.
- **Joke confetti:** adding a punchline to every paragraph until the insight
  has to queue behind the writer’s personality.
- **The source recital:** narrating where a conclusion came from instead of
  letting the conclusion do its job.
- **The empty identity:** using a grand label such as “aggressive” or
  “versatile” without a recognisable Dota behaviour underneath it.

## Chapter checks

- **Summary:** Can the player name their leading shape without opening another
  chapter?
- **Elements:** Does each tile feel like a piece of the player rather than a
  metric definition?
- **Patterns:** Does the relationship arrive as a useful “that is me” moment?
- **Heroes:** Does the portfolio make the hero choices feel connected, with one
  practical next experiment?
- **You:** Does the Mirror feel like a reveal, not a personality diagnosis?

Keep privacy, errors, destructive actions, permissions, and sharing literal.
When a finding cannot resolve, use a short human state and move on; do not
fill the space with a smaller-print explanation of the gate.
